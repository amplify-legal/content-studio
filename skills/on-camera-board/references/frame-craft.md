# Frame craft

> How to make a video better *because* it is on a canvas, rather than just moving the slides to
> a new tool. Read this before designing a frame set.

## The one thing to understand

A slide deck is a list. A canvas is a place. If the video still walks a list, moving to a canvas
bought nothing and cost you your brand font.

Everything below follows from that.

## Where this came from

3 things in the raw footage of a first attempt pointed straight at a canvas, which is why this
is not a preference argument.

- The editor, twice, at 37 minutes into the recording: **"too many cuts."** Pans and zooms are
  continuous. They replace hard cuts with motion the viewer's eye follows, so the same content
  needs fewer edits.
- The editor again, in the first minute: **"you can even look at your screen, it's more
  educational, like a teacher. Circle."** He was directing the creator toward annotation that
  an HTML deck structurally could not accept.
- The creator, at 48 minutes: **"14 hours in red, then transitions to 7 hours in green."** He
  was describing an animation, because the static page could not change while he talked.

## The 6 moves

**1. Build the anchor frame first, and keep coming back to it.**

The strongest structural change available. Instead of "here are 3 problems", draw the path the
thing actually takes through the business: a lead arrives, the work gets done, the invoice goes
out, the money lands. Then hang each problem off the stage where it happens. The video becomes a
tour of one picture rather than a list of 3 things, and the viewer holds the whole shape in
their head the entire time.

**2. Zoom is emphasis, so spend it like emphasis.**

Talk close on one number and it feels big. Pull out to the anchor frame and it is one of 4.
Physical scale does emotional work a number on a slide cannot. Use it at the seams between
sections, never mid-sentence.

**3. Leave holes on purpose.**

Ship a frame half-empty, with red-bordered blanks next to your own numbers, and fill in the
viewer's live on camera. **This is the move HTML structurally cannot make**, and it flips the
viewer from watching your arithmetic to doing their own. If only one canvas technique survives
in your setup, make it this one.

**4. Destroy something on screen.**

Put the claim you disagree with on the frame so it can be crossed out in front of the viewer.
Killing an idea visually lands harder than arguing against it.

**5. Give the board away.**

Share a duplicate-able copy in the description: here is the map, fill in your own numbers. The
lead magnet becomes the video itself, and anyone who fills it in has already done the first half
of the work you would have done with them on a call. Note that an editable-board ceiling applies
to your account, not to theirs; a copy lands in the viewer's own.

**6. Build a piece of it live.**

Dragging one marker onto the map while talking is worth more than the same marker fading in. It
reads as thinking rather than presenting.

## Restraint rules

Presentation tools earn motion-sickness reputations by zooming because they can. 4 rules keep
this the good version.

1. Never rotate. Pan and zoom only.
2. Move on a beat, never mid-sentence.
3. 2 zoom levels, map and detail. A third loses people.
4. Always land square on a frame. No resting at an angle or between frames.

## On-camera copy

The creator's rule, said about an HTML deck and governing a canvas identically:

> *"Remove all the subtitles and little notes. I want big letters, easy to read, not so many
> words that are distracting, only headlines and numbers. No small text."*

Headlines and numbers only. No subtitles, no captions, no footnotes, no small print.

## Learned from real edits

These came out of creators re-cutting machine-built frame sets by hand before recording. Take
them as guidelines, because each came from one video, and one example yields a guideline rather
than a hard rule.

- **Fewer things per frame, but the type gets bigger.** If something needs shrinking to fit, cut
  it instead.
- **Labels that name what the viewer can already see go first.** The creator is the
  explanation, said out loud.
- **One purpose per frame.** When a frame does 2 jobs, split it rather than compress it. In one
  re-cut, both new frames came from splits, and the specific fault was a frame that carried both
  a demo prompt and the call to action for a different video.
- **3 of something is often 2.** 3 problem chips, 4 numbers and 4 price rows all lost one.
- **Ranges beat precision on camera.** "200 to 300" beats "about 280".
- **Keep only the blanks tied to a question asked aloud.** Of 5 fill-in blanks in one board, 1
  survived, 2 were parked off-frame and 2 were permanently answered.
- **Stacking vertically works.** Frames in a vertical column with a heavy dark arrow between
  each pair, each frame sized to its content rather than to a fixed 16:9, reads as one flow
  rather than as a deck. Section titles then live *between* the frames, on the arrows, as
  deliberately off-brand markers: scaffolding for the presenter, not content for the camera.
- **Colour gets louder than the brand sheet.** Brighter reds, brighter greens. Whether that
  feeds back into the brand sheet itself is an open question.
- **Hand strokes land on arithmetic and mechanism.** In one recording all 18 of them landed on
  the cost comparison and the system diagram, and none on a frame that only made a statement.
  That is a useful signal for which frames deserve the space to be drawn on, and which frames
  are not earning their place.

- **The board is a prop for what is said out loud, not a document.** A board can be correct in
  every detail and still be impossible to deliver: dense frames read as hard to perform. Build for
  the take, not for completeness.
- **Some formats want a different surface entirely.** A ranking video (a tier list, a
  head-to-head) can perform better from an interactive page where items are dragged into place
  live than from static frames. When the format is a ranking, ask whether the board is the right
  surface before building it.
- **Real logos, never drawn ones.** When a frame names a product, put its actual logo on it, all
  logos normalised to one square size so none dominates by accident.

## Before the take, every time

- Click through the frame set once, out loud. A frame you cannot talk over for 30 seconds is
  wrong.
- Check every frame against `content/taste/must-dos.md`: whatever a recording must show on screen
  (a resource, a mid-video ask) has a frame or a slot on one.
- Read the board against the approved script: same order, same numbering, every number on screen
  matches the number said aloud.

## Open

- A frame set that reads beautifully in the preview page can still die on camera. The only real
  test is the creator clicking through it out loud before the take.
- Nobody has proved the 6 moves against a controlled A/B. They come from watching raw footage
  and from canvas-storytelling principle, and the first takes that used them corrected 2 of the
  rules above. Expect yours to correct more.
