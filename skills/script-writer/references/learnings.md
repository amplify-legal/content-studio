# Learnings

What past scripts taught, kept as rules with the reason attached. Read this before writing a
script. Add to it after every script the creator reacts to.

**An entry holds 4 things:** the date, what happened, the rule that follows, and what it cost to
learn. The cost is the part that makes a rule stick, so never drop it. One example gives you a
guideline; the same thing happening twice gives you a rule.

---

## The nuance principle

When a video evaluates or ranks anything, every item gets an honest upside **and** an honest
limitation. Never shut something down completely, even the thing that finishes last.

This is the single strongest credibility move available in a ranking video. Without it the video
reads as a sales pitch for whatever wins. With it, it reads as an expert evaluating, and the
viewer trusts the ranking enough to act on it.

The pattern per item:

1. Name plus its rank, on screen
2. The scores, each with a one-line reason
3. The real numbers
4. The case for it, meant honestly
5. The case against it, meant honestly
6. Their own take, 15 to 30 seconds spoken

---

## A ranking format doubles as the argument

In a tier list or a ranking, the structure of the list is the structure of the argument. The
finished ranking shown at the top is the thesis, each evaluation is a supporting argument, the
honest pro and con is the credibility, the numbers are the evidence, and whatever stands alone
at the top is the conclusion.

Order matters more than it looks. Open with the item everybody relates to, so trust is built
before anything contentious. Put the payoff item third or so, once 2 balanced evaluations have
earned the right to make a claim. Everything after the payoff gets evaluated through its lens.
End on the item that leaves the viewer thinking rather than the one that scores lowest.

---

## Absolute numbers, never a bare percentage

A percentage with no absolute context is a lie the viewer catches half a second later. "21 to 71
times your money back" sounds incredible until they work out it is a tool that costs less than
a lunch a month.

So whenever a number does work in a script, give all of it: what goes in over a year, what comes
back over a year, the difference, and at what size of operation. Give it at 2 sizes when the
answer changes with scale, because the viewer needs to find themselves in it.

---

## Write the visuals after the script is stable

Every visual written before the script was finished had to be thrown away, because the content
moved while the script was being written. Scores changed, the pros and cons got sharper, numbers
got added.

Script first. Stabilise the content. Then build the boards, cards and artifacts against what the
script actually says.

**Cost of learning it:** 13 artifact prompts written twice.

---

## The hook that works: reframe plus proof

The opening that landed did 2 things in 2 sentences. It named the criteria explicitly, and it
separated itself from every other video on the topic with a proof frame.

The shape: "I ranked every X on one thing: how much money it actually makes. Not in theory. What
has been proven."

Show the finished result for about 3 seconds, then take it away. The viewer now has a question
they need answered, which is what carries them past the first minute.

**Never open with "hey guys, in today's video".** It kills click-through on a cold audience,
because it is the sound of a video that has not started yet.

---

## Credentials go where they are earned, not in the opener

The authority line ("I watched this exact pattern play out 10 years ago in a different field")
belongs inside the section where it explains something, not stapled to the front of the video.

In the run this came from, the contrarian data point led naturally to "so why do they fail?",
which led to "I have seen this before". The credential arrived as the answer to a question the
viewer was already asking, which is the only place it is not bragging.

---

## Real and verified

Every story in a script is a real one with real detail. Name the person when they have agreed to
be named; otherwise describe them by shape ("a 12-person shop that lost its weekend orders").
"One of my clients" with nothing after it is not a story, it is a placeholder, and viewers hear
it as one.

Verify every number with the creator before it goes into a script, not after. An outline
carrying a figure marked "need to verify" is a figure that will otherwise be said on camera.

---

## Shorter sections when the count is high

A 12-minute video with 7 items plus one deep dive gives each of the other 6 items 30 to 45
seconds. At that length, every word has to advance the argument. Cut anything that repeats,
restates, or warms up.

---

## Bullets say what to say next

**What happened.** A script arrived with the sourcing, the rulings and the machine's notes
written inside the bullets. The creator could not read it as a script on camera.

**The rule.** At most 2 lines of italic context at the top of a section, only where they need
it. Then bullets that are the things to say, in the order they say them. Provenance lives in the
brief.

**Cost.** A rewrite of the whole body on the morning of the recording.

---

## The format lives in one file

**What happened.** The creator had described the shape they record from (written hook, guide
bullets, verbatim key lines, offered transitions) many times in conversation. A script still
arrived in the wrong shape on recording morning, because the shape lived in chat and nowhere a
session would read it.

**The rule.** The shape lives in `content/taste/script-format.md`, and every skill that writes a
recording script conforms to that file. A correction to the shape goes into that file the same
session.

**Cost.** A recording morning spent reformatting instead of rehearsing.

---

## Fix the beat, not the script

A request to fix one beat touches that beat only. Regenerating the whole draft to improve one
section quietly replaces the parts that were working, and the creator has to re-check
everything instead of one paragraph.

---

## A story fits the viewer, not the drama

**What happened.** 2 rounds of story options were built on the most dramatic moments in the
creator's life. Both were rejected, because the viewer was never going to be in any of those
situations, so the story could not help them avoid anything.

**The rule.** Before reaching for a story, ask whether the person this video is for would see
themselves in it. Pick the ordinary, recognisable moment over the dramatic one.

**Cost.** 2 full rounds of options thrown away.

---

## Scope is asked, never inferred

**What happened.** The creator said "3 problems". The machine read that as 3 episodes and
briefed only the first. They meant one episode covering all 3.

**The rule.** One video or several, and what is in each, is part of the map. Ask it in the
first exchange.

**Cost.** A brief for the wrong video.

---

## A deletion is a decision

**What happened.** A session found a brief the creator had deleted, recovered it from history,
and started building from it. They had deleted it on purpose.

**The rule.** Never reconstruct killed work from version history or from a stale pointer. Killed
work gets a row in `content/graveyard.md` with the reason, and the graveyard is read every run.

**Cost.** A session's work built on something the creator had already rejected.

---

## Every promise on camera is a live link

**What happened.** A video promised a free resource twice on tape. The resource existed and was
live, but the person writing the description did not know where it was, flagged the link as
"to find", and the video shipped without it. A viewer read it as clickbait and cancelled a call
they had booked.

**The rule.** The script lists every on-camera promise. Before the video publishes, each one is
a URL in the description that has been fetched and loads. An open flag in a description file is
not a gate; the launch step checks each promise against the description itself.

**Cost.** A booked conversation lost, and the trust that came with it.

---

## No live date said on camera

A date that will be stale by the time the video is watched turns the video into an expired ad.
The current date goes in the description or a pinned comment, where it can be updated. The same
goes for any real contact's name on screen: that is somebody else's privacy in a file that never
comes back down.

---

## Re-read immediately before you write

**What happened.** 2 sessions interviewed the creator about the same video on the same day and
wrote 2 competing briefs. Both had checked for an existing brief at the start; the first one was
written after the second had already started.

**The rule.** Announce the work in `content/in-flight.md` when you start. That is a map, not a
gate. The check that actually prevents the duplicate is re-reading the briefs folder immediately
before you write the file. When 2 versions exist anyway, merge them and mark the real conflicts
`OPEN:` rather than picking.

**Cost.** An hour of the creator's interview time, spent twice.

---

## A recording slot with nothing briefed is a decision

**What happened.** A recording block sat on the calendar with no brief behind it, and the plan
for the day listed it as an ordinary calendar row. The creator sat down to record with nothing
shaped.

**The rule.** A recording block with no brief behind it goes to the top of the day's plan as
"you record on <day> and nothing is briefed", and the interview opens the same day.

**Cost.** A recording slot used to plan instead of record.

---

## How to add to this file

After a script the creator reacted to:

1. What did they change? Write the change.
2. Why did they change it? Ask them, in the same session. The answer is the rule.
3. Where does the rule belong? If it is about how they sound, it goes in their voice file. If it
   is about how a script gets built, it goes here.
4. What did it cost? An extra hour, a re-record, a video that underperformed. Write it down.

Keep this file to about 20 entries. When it grows past that, prune whatever a newer entry has
already replaced.
