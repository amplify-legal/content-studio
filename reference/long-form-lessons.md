# Long-form lessons

> Installed to `content/reference/long-form-lessons.md` and read by the `long-form-editor` skill. Companion files: `content/reference/workshop-edit.md` (recorded sessions with attendees and a deck), `content/reference/podcast-edit.md` and `content/reference/podcast-edit-runbook.md` (conversational episodes), and `content/reference/lessons.md` (the rules that cross every lane, including publishing). This is where the taste lives: what makes a long edit watchable rather than merely correct, the shapes that work, and every error that has actually been paid for on a long recording.
>
> **How to read the numbers.** Every measurement below came off a real tape on a real pipeline. Where something was never measured, it says so. A number with no source is a number somebody guessed, and there are none of those in here on purpose.

## Part 1: what makes a long edit watchable

A short is watchable because of what is in it. A long form is watchable because of what is **between** things. Almost every finding in this file is about a seam, a gap, a hand-off or a transition, and almost none of it is about the content.

### The 5 things that make a raw recording feel raw

1. **Dead air.** Not the natural 1 to 3 second pauses, which are part of the feel and stay. The 4 second ones where somebody is thinking, and the digital silences where a tool is loading. On one audited cut there was a 4.7 second stretch of true digital silence, momentary loudness at -74 dBFS, while a spinner on screen read "asking a question, 14m 12s".
2. **A picture that does not move.** Measured across one whole runtime: **41.6% of it sat inside runs of 10 seconds or more where nothing visibly changed**, including a 101 second stretch where a shared screen never moved at all. Nothing in a normal check battery finds this. It is what "slow" actually is.
3. **Unannounced jumps.** 12 of 33 cuts on one audited cut jumped between 17 and 330 raw seconds with no bridge. 11 of those 12 sat exactly on a chapter mark, which is the proof that each one was a new teaching beat that needed announcing rather than a trim.
4. **Sentences that do not finish.** Of those same 33 cuts: 13 left mid-sentence, 12 landed mid-sentence, and 8 fell inside a word.
5. **An ending that is not an ending.** One cut reached its real closing line at 12:38, then ran 42 more seconds and stopped mid-breath.

Fix those 5 and a raw recording becomes watchable without a single thing being added to it.

### Correct is not the same as watchable

A cut can pass every mechanical check and still be wrong, and the faults a person names first are almost always sitting on top of a mechanical cause. One person watched a cut once and said 3 things: the captions look weird, the layout could be better, and it is too jarring. Every one of those turned out to be a specific, findable mechanism:

- "The captions look weird" was a highlight colour running backwards, because of a missing token.
- "The layout could be better" was equal-sized boxes giving a third of the picture to somebody who never speaks in the cut.
- "Too jarring" was 12 unannounced jumps and 33 cuts that mostly did not land on a sentence.

**So take a vague reaction seriously and go looking for the mechanism.** "It feels off" is usually a measurement away from being actionable.

## Part 2: the shapes that work

### How a workshop or training opens

- **Drop the pre-show entirely.** On one recording that was the first 9 minutes, everything before the host's first real line. Open on the host's first real words ("good afternoon everyone, and welcome"), never on pre-show banter.
- **The deck is on screen inside the first 15 seconds**, even while people are still talking over it.
- **The faces stay on the opening.** Never a still of the presentation where a face could be. The layout that came out of this: faces top left, the deck shifted right to make room.
- **An animated opener works because of the resolve, not because of the animation.** Render the opener into the exact slot the live footage will occupy, and dissolve it onto a **real frame of what comes next**. Then the hand-off is invisible and the only thing that changes at the cut is one face appearing.
- **One measured trade worth knowing.** On a tape whose screen share did not start for 23 minutes, the house rule "deck on screen inside 15 seconds" could not be honoured. The answer was not to bend the rule quietly: it was 3 priced options put to the person whose cut it was, and they picked "cut the cold open to one story", which got the screen on at about 0:50 instead of 1:20.

### How a conversational episode opens

- **A trailer, not a teaser.** A chain of micro-clips, 1 to 5 seconds each, hard cut, 25 to 45 seconds total, with a hard cap around 30 to 40 seconds. The bar is energy and impact, not a new structure.
- **Nobody introduces themselves inside it.** The introduction comes after.
- **It closes on suspense**, hard cut right before a reveal.
- **A laugh is a pickable moment on its own**, not filler between quotes.
- **Inside a trailer, a clip ends on the last word's decay, not on a policy gap.** The body's silence policy is too loose for a montage: a 0.6 second tail on a 3 second clip is a fifth of it. Anything over about 0.15 seconds gets trimmed. This one came directly from somebody saying the trailer felt awkward and being asked what they meant: "half-second lingering silences and the choice of clips".
- **The trailer ships standalone, first**, as its own file, before the long render starts. 3 passes shipped as "a bit awkward" because self-review kept passing it and the person only ever saw it welded to 28 minutes.
- **Bring options, not a version.** Several trailers and several music beds, and the person picks.

### The shape of a condensed cut

The single most useful thing in this file, recovered from the sessions that made a 14 minute cut out of a 50 minute training.

- **A condensed cut is pure subtraction, in the tape's own order.** Every version of the measured cut ascends monotonically in raw seconds. **Nothing was ever re-sequenced.** That is exactly what makes "render a draft and let them subtract" workable, because they can point at a minute and say drop it. The moment you re-order, they have to hold the whole thing in their head instead.
- **The draft rule: one unit per beat, plus the call to action.**
- **The only things ever added** were a rendered opener and one still frame.
- **Name what you dropped, in the handover.** The real line: "left out on purpose: the chat-reading moments, the tier list, 2 tangents, the whole question and answer section. Those are yours to put back."
- **Under 15 minutes for a condensed training.** The person's own words: "14 is good, generally under 15 for these things is good." The 12 they first asked for was a target, not a ceiling, and finishing every slide build beat hitting the number.
- **Every slide shows all its value, not every detail.** Their words: "when you tell a story well, that includes showing all the value that is in each slide, without needing to show every detail that is in each slide."
- **Close on the viewer, not on the room.** One cut was found ending on "so, what's on your mind?", a question to people the viewer is not among. It moved to "I think we're all aligned on that. Cool."
- **When finishing every build breaks the target, put the trade to them with the candidates priced.** The message that worked named the 3 slides that could come out to get back to 12 minutes, each with its duration in seconds. That is what produced "14 is good, generally under 15". Without the priced list the machine would have silently cut something they wanted.

### Where the energy dies in a raw recording, and what to do

| Where | What to do |
|---|---|
| Long thinking pauses | Tighten. This is the thing that got named as worth replicating: "super clean cuts, I really like how you skipped some of the long-thinking moments and kept the flow going" |
| A list read out as a litany | Trim litanies before you trim stories. A trim that breaks a sentence's weave does not happen for a few seconds' gain |
| The question and answer section | Often off the condensed cut entirely. It is usually the best material for shorts and the worst material for a condensed long form |
| A slide held with nothing moving | Either cut it shorter or give it a reveal. Measure it with a frame difference, do not guess |
| Post-show tape, after "we'll edit this part out" | **A quarry, not garbage.** One episode's outro insert and its sign-off both came from after that line |
| Somebody repeating the same sentence 3 times | Usually keep one, the cleanest or most emphatic take. Different from a rhetorical rule of 3, which you keep whole |

## Part 3: errors not to repeat

Every row is a real failure on a real long recording, with what it cost. This is the most valuable thing in this file.

### The privacy errors, and they cost the most

| Error | Rule |
|---|---|
| **2 frames cleared a 2 minute screen share** | A build note read "their own tabs only, checked at full resolution", off 2 frames of the tab strip. A full-resolution 1fps pass across the same stretch then found an address bar dropdown carrying a private email subject, dashboard rows with account identifiers, a logged-in social feed with notification badges, search history, and the tab strip and address bar on **every** frame. Sample a share at 1fps at full resolution before the first render, and assume the browser belongs to whoever was presenting. |
| **A frame cleared as "clean, no names anywhere"** | It carried an uncleared attendee's face and name in a webcam tile. A second carried an undimmed sidebar of private conversation titles. The clearing was done by somebody who believed they had checked. Where the recording is a screen share with a webcam column, cropping the column off is usually the whole fix. |
| **A layout hand-off measured to the half second** | The real hand-off was 0.38 seconds later than the number written down. Those 10 frames put raw, unblurred attendee gallery into the deck slot of the delivered cut. The person who watched it called it "a weird flicker around second 26". Measure every hand-off **to the frame**, by stepping through the second around it. |
| **A layout seam that moves mid-tape** | A shared screen 1669px wide until about 29:30 and 1621px after it, because a participant strip widened. A crop fixed at the first measurement leaked 48px of attendee tiles across the whole back half. Measure the seam every 10 seconds, take the **narrowest** value for the whole cut, and lose the window chrome instead. |
| **A blur that is on when the private thing is absent** | 95% of one cut's sidebar-blurred runtime had no sidebar on screen, because the detector fired on any numbered list. Roughly 90 of 800 seconds of teaching content was smeared, the worst being 27 seconds of the closing answer. A blur with nothing behind it is a fault, not a safety margin. |
| **Hand-editing blur windows off an audit list** | Removing the false-positive windows by hand then dropped a window covering a genuinely open sidebar listing 15 private conversation titles. **Derive the windows**: OCR each band at 2fps across every kept second and emit a window only where private text is actually legible. On that tape: 7 windows totalling 70 seconds, against a hand list carrying 142 seconds of blur over a closed sidebar. |
| **Sampling an exposure check every 5 seconds** | A 1.8 second exposure of a private list survived an OCR sweep at 5 seconds. **Exposure checks sample at 1 second or finer.** |
| **A deck carries the audience it was built for** | A talk recorded for one profession was condensed for another. The spoken vocabulary screened clean, 0 hits across all 10 units. One phrase survived anyway because it was **never spoken**: it was printed on a slide and on screen for 11 seconds. Screen the pixels as well as the words. |
| **Your own workspace blurred in a moving recording** | Every blur failure above applies to it. When your own screen, an app or a dashboard has to appear on camera, rebuild the page clean (people replaced by who they are, clients removed, figures replaced by ones of the same shape), read its rendered text and its HTML comments, and record the rebuild. Attendees cannot be rebuilt, so for them the blur stays. Full rule: `content/reference/lessons.md`, rule 9. |

### The cut and boundary errors

| Error | Rule |
|---|---|
| **A boundary snapped to the transcript's word spans** | Transcribers hang the trailing pause on the word, so snapping to a word's end puts the dead air straight back in. Snap to **measured audio energy**: 20ms RMS windows on 16kHz mono, threshold -38 dBFS, 3 consecutive quiet windows, walking outward in 20ms steps, capped at 1.5s, 0.05s pad. |
| **A cut that sliced a word in half** | A corrected opening cut landed at 610.00 and left half of the word "opportunity" behind. The plan looked right and the deep pass was green, because neither checks whether a word survived. **Read the rendered captions back across every join.** The boundary moved to 610.55. |
| **A caption filter keyed on the word's END** | The transcriber reported a word's span running 1.2 seconds past when it was actually spoken, because it attached the following pause to the word. A cut placed on measured quiet left the word fully audible and the caption builder threw it away. **Keep a word whose START is inside the segment**, and let its highlight run through a contiguous seam. |
| **A cut that left a slide before its build finished** | A slide reading "5 actions is all it takes" was cut before the fourth action appeared. The spine was picked off the transcript and **a transcript cannot see a reveal.** Build the reveal timeline from the frames and extend any unit that ends inside a build. |
| **Dropping a card the presenter never got to** | Where a reveal was clicked late, after the words, the cut shows the finished slide **as the words land**, off a real frame of it. A card the presenter never reached is the editor's to reveal, never to drop. |
| **A frame-quantised segment with independently cut audio** | A 25fps segment can come out a frame longer or shorter than asked. On one cut, 118 of them drifted the audio by seconds. Cut the audio to each segment's **real frame count**, read back from the rendered file with `ffprobe`, sample-exact, with 30ms fades at every edge. |
| **A chapter timestamp derived from the pre-speed timeline** | The cut renders at 1.05x, so chapters derived from a 1.0x edit list are 5% late, which on a 46 minute cut is over 2 minutes by the end. Caught with 34 wrong chapter lines already drafted into 2 descriptions. **Any timestamp handed to a human comes off the delivered file.** |
| **A silence map that cuts through quiet words** | Tightening every stretch below -38 dB clipped 5 spoken words in a 9 minute cut ("we saved them hundreds of" became "contract. Thousands of dollars."). **A pause is cut only BETWEEN 2 transcript words**, and only where at least 0.6 seconds of that gap is measured quiet. Any builder that trims pauses off a silence list alone has this fault. |
| **A false start stripped of its dash** | A transcript false start ("c-", "jo-") is dropped from the captions entirely, never stripped of its dash, or it shows as a stray letter. |
| **`silencedetect` returning nothing** | That means a noisy room, not a clean cut. Diagnose at a higher threshold (-35dB or -30dB) rather than assuming there is nothing to trim. And never drop the threshold toward -30dB on a clean tape, because it eats soft word endings. |

### The caption errors, all 4 mechanical

| Error | Rule |
|---|---|
| **The karaoke highlight ran BACKWARDS** | Emitting a colour transition without pinning the base colour first makes the renderer resolve the first transition's target for any time before its start, so a line arrives with **every word already highlighted** and the colour drains left to right as the words are spoken. The highlight marks what has **not** been said. Proved on a bench render: at 0.2 seconds into a 4 word line, all 4 words were highlighted. The fix is one token, the base colour pinned before the transitions. It was the first thing the viewer's eye caught. |
| **A word count doing the line breaking** | A hard 4 word cap put **524 of 705 lines at exactly 4 words** and read "So I was in / an instance where, a / firm was trying to". Break where **language** breaks: score every candidate boundary (sentence end, clause, a real pause, a speaker change) and choose the whole set with a shortest path, never greedily. Greedy leaves 3 word fragments after every forced break. |
| **"one" converted to a numeral** | "one" is a pronoun. A numerals rule that converts it ships "the big 1,", "there's the second 1.", "see them 1 by 1", **15 times in one cut**. A small number only becomes a digit when a unit word follows it. |
| **2 sentences welded into one caption card** | 5 cards on one cut carried the tail of one sentence and the head of the next as a single unreadable line. Finish the sentence, then cut. |
| **Captions over the speakers' mouths** | The caption block never crosses a mouth on any layout. Check a full-resolution frame of each speaker talking. Name cards give way and go lower, even overlapping the frame edge; captions do not move up to make room for them. |
| **Word gaps measured in PIL, drawn by libass** | A per-word layout that ignores the style's letter spacing and outlines shrinks the visible gap to about 7px, and a line reads as one run-on word. Add both to the measured advance; about 26px visible is right. A group wider than about 1500px on a 1920 frame becomes 2 caption frames. |
| **Crosstalk captioned as if it were the floor** | On a 2-presenter recording, "yes" and "I see" said under the other person's sentence are the main caption fault. Read every caption against who is speaking before the review. |

### The layout errors

| Error | Rule |
|---|---|
| **A picture-in-picture hard-wired to one person** | For 3 minutes 32 seconds of a screen share the only face on screen was one person's, including roughly 48 seconds where somebody else was teaching. The layout follows the voice, and the speaker timeline is knowable from diarization plus the name baked into the active tile. |
| **Equal boxes** | They give the loudest real estate to whoever is silent. On one cut, somebody who never speaks once in the kept material held an equal box through the whole opening, carrying a competing wordmark and then a frozen photograph. Check who actually speaks **in the kept material** before designing the layout. |
| **Upscaling a tile past its ceiling** | A 384x216 gallery tile upscales cleanly to 2x, acceptably to 2.5x (960x540), and mushily at 3x. Design inside that or the hero face is soft. |
| **A still of a speaker taken from the recording** | It reads as their video frozen, not as a photo. Use a portrait-shaped photo instead, 4:5, mouth closed, eyes to camera, name and title beside it. Look in your own archive before the web; a clean frame from the same tape is the last resort, and say which it is. |
| **A slow push-in on a still** | A zoom filter resamples the frame by a fraction of a pixel every frame, and on flat ground, type and a face shimmer. It reads as a broken file. Measured: median 11 levels of per-frame change in the picture area with spikes to 223, against 0 to 2 once the still was left alone. Test any still with `tblend=all_mode=difference,signalstats` and read YMAX. |
| **A shared window assumed to be the deck** | On one livestream the browser shrank twice, zoomed out for about 45 seconds, and once showed a new-tab page with bookmarks and a local file path. Match every half second of the share against renders of every build state of the deck: under about 8/255 mean difference, repair a covered corner from the render; 8 to 30, show the clean render; 30 and over, hold the last good slide. |
| **A layout switch treated as a cut** | A stream's talk-to-deck switch is a 0.4 to 0.6 second crossfade (talk-to-deck fades before the switch, deck-to-talk after it). Measure each at full frame rate and hold the last clean frame, or faces ghost inside the deck for 5 to 8 frames. |
| **Slide changes sampled every half second** | Puts the wrong build on screen for up to 0.8 seconds at every change, 28 times on a 45 minute stream. Re-measure every change to the frame. |
| **An RGB still laid onto a BT.709 tape** | Converted with BT.601 unless told otherwise, which put a greenish box on 38 of 45 minutes of deck. Convert every PNG patch or slide with `scale=out_color_matrix=bt709:out_range=tv,format=yuv420p` and measure the rendered frame against the pixels beside it. |
| **A slide-led short showing whatever was on screen** | A faithful timeline showed the agenda slide while the presenter explained a different point, so the picture contradicted the words. Pin the slide per piece. And where a stream's own graphics fade over the camera at a layout switch, a piece ending inside one freezes its picture and keeps the audio. |
| **A layout probe reused across tapes** | A rule reading "it is a screen share if the left region is brighter than 110" read **every 2 shot** on a different tape as a share, because that person's wall was bright. A layout probe is a property of the tape it was measured on. Every recording gets a fresh scan, checked against a contact sheet. |

### The render and pipeline errors

| Error | Rule |
|---|---|
| **"The render finished" taken as "the change landed"** | An edit that misses its target block produces a clean render that changed nothing. One version came back **byte for byte identical** to the previous one, and only a file size comparison caught it. The cause was 2 identical keys in the same dictionary, so the edit landed in the block the language discards. Compare every output against the previous output before delivering it. |
| **A geometry change against a warm segment cache** | Rebuilding only what changed is the right economy: when a blur changed, every deck segment was kept and only gallery segments were re-rendered, so an 11 minute and a 46 minute cut both came back in 12 minutes. But a **geometry** change invalidates every segment, and the cache has to be deleted deliberately. |
| **A segment render sitting at 0% CPU** | That is a deadlock, not a slow render. One 1.5 second segment hung for 13 minutes because its audio ran out exactly where a padding filter began, so the graph never flushed. With 1.0 second of slack on the input window the same segment rendered in 0.4 seconds. The structural fix: `-nostdin` on every call, a bounded wait of 60 seconds plus 3x the segment duration, and one retry. The tell is "nothing is growing" while an ffmpeg is still listed: check `ps` for its CPU. |
| **A filter probed by re-rendering** | Probe a filter as a **still PNG** first. One blur radius was chosen from 2 still probes rather than 2 renders, and 2 ffmpeg failures were paid for on the way to learning that the radius caps are silent and low. |
| **Silent filter caps** | `boxblur` caps `luma_radius` at 24 and `chroma_radius` at half the chroma height of the region, which on a 48px name strip is 12. Exceeding either fails the render with no useful message. 2 render failures paid for this one. |
| **A colon inside a filter label** | It silently fails the whole filter graph. Escape it (`00\:30`). This is worse than an error, because it makes a verification step produce nothing while looking like it ran. |
| **A seeked input composited onto a looped background** | The seeked input carries the **tape's** timestamps, so the overlay reads the main input's clock, the tile frames are permanently in the future, and the output is a bare background with the furniture on it and no people. **ffmpeg exits 0.** Every overlaid chain starts with `setpts=PTS-STARTPTS`. |
| **A looped still image with no explicit duration** | It never ends. ffmpeg keeps encoding duplicate frames after the video finishes until the disk or the kernel stops it, and it looks exactly like running out of memory at "98%". Every `-loop 1` input carries an explicit `-t`, plus `shortest=1` on the first overlay. |
| **`timeout` on macOS** | It does not exist, and every step using it exits 127. `perl -e "alarm N; exec @ARGV"` is the portable version. |
| **A drawbox fill reasoned about rather than measured** | A fill is converted to limited-range colour and comes back darker. A near-white card measuring 253 patched with `0xFDFDFD` decodes to 250, and 3 levels on near-white is a visible block at 2x zoom. Encoder quality makes no difference, which is what proves it is the colour and not the encode. Only pure white lands within one level. Measure the fill by rendering candidates. |
| **A preview rendered through a different code path** | 3 layout candidates were judged with a mock caption drawn in plain white, while the shipping pipeline's real captions were coloured the whole time. The note came back: "in the screenshots you shared I don't see the accent colour, but I do like when that is the case." A preview that reimplements the composition is a different product with the same name. **One module holds every number, and the preview frame is produced by the shipping render function itself.** |
| **Judging a long render's health by the CPU meter** | Working means the **output file is growing**. The CPU is high both when ffmpeg is encoding and when it is spinning on a stall. |
| **A name fixed downstream of the transcriber** | Bias the transcriber with the recording's own list of names and terms (`--keyterms-file`). Measured on one tape: the plain model returned 2 different wrong spellings of the same 2 names across 2 versions; the same model with a key-terms list returned both correctly. A find-and-replace cannot catch a string you have not seen yet. And the transcribers cache on the filename, so a re-run with a new list needs `--force` or it serves the old transcript forever. |
| **The wrong transcription engine, or an unnamed one** | Engine order: **ElevenLabs Scribe `scribe_v2`** by default, **Deepgram `nova-3`** as the declared fallback (same key-terms file), **local whisper** last, for no network and no key (it takes no key terms). On the one tape all 3 ran on, the confirmed proper-noun sites scored 17/17, 16/17 and 11/17. The engine that ran is named in the output file's header, never a silent default. A cut on Deepgram words uses word STARTS (its ends run into the next word), and its numbers arrive as words, so captions need a number pass. |
| **A music bed judged by ear** | A bed 6 to 7 dB under the voices was "way too loud"; about 19 to 20 dB under was right. Measure bed against voice in the same window and say the number before anybody watches. |
| **Chunk bounds off the frame grid** | A parallel finalise that speeds up each chunk loses up to a frame per chunk when a chunk's sped-up length is not a whole number of frames, and sample-exact audio runs ahead (121ms on one cut). Round every bound to the output frame grid, and bound the chunks on the **real master**, never on edit-list arithmetic (an EDL 0.19 seconds short cut the last chunk short). |
| **A memory gate on swap used** | The OS keeps swap allocated after the pressure is gone, so a gate on swap used waited on a machine that was 49% free. Gate on memory pressure. |

### The review errors

| Error | Rule |
|---|---|
| **A 100 frame contact sheet called a review** | 100 screenshots of a 15 minute video gives a general idea and finds none of the faults above. The instruction, in the reviewer's own words: "you need to adversarially attack different vectors of the video itself, including the layouts, transitions and captions. For that you need more specialised sets of screenshots that are closer together." One dense pass **per vector**, each with its own sampling rate. |
| **One agent reviewing every vector** | An independent pass per vector, so nobody is marking their own homework. And the end-of-run review is a separate reviewer that reads the whole conversation, never the session reviewing itself. |
| **5 visual passes and no audio pass** | Dead air and a 2.2 LU speaker imbalance were found only after a pass that listened to the audio alone, having been missed by 5 visual passes. Audio gets its own pass. |
| **Spot samples called a deep pass** | A shallow pass once cleared a cut carrying a 0.3 second lip-sync drift at the end. A deep pass is every-N-second frames across the **whole** runtime plus freeze and black detection plus an audio and video duration check. |
| **A check that fails on a file you know is right** | The check is wrong. 2 of these: a probe reading a title card's last frame as empty because it sampled 0.03 seconds in instead of 0.20, and a 2 pixel probe landing in a gap between layout regions. Fix the check; do not accept the finding. |
| **A failure cause asserted rather than proved** | 3 consecutive confident misdiagnoses once hid a self-inflicted missing flag for 3 days, with the machine's owner closing browsers and blaming their own hardware. **A failure cause is a hypothesis until an artifact proves it, and your own command gets audited first.** Say "hypothesis" out loud until it reproduces. |

## Part 4: render discipline at long-form length

`render-reliability.md` in this folder covers the 8 things that kill a long render and is the file to read first. What changes at long-form length:

**Everything is a pool of segments, so everything is cacheable, and a stale cache is a new failure mode.** Content-key the segments so a retry only redoes what is missing. Delete the cache deliberately on a geometry change. And compare the output against the previous output, because a cache that silently served an old segment looks identical to a render that worked.

**Measure the parallel width, do not reason about it.** On one machine, **4 parallel encodes beat 8** (2.12x realtime against 1.25x), and an earlier benchmark saying 8 was faster had been taken under a completely different load. A 60 second measurement answers this in 2 minutes. On the same machine: a 21 minute master finalised in about 10 minutes clear and about 25 minutes under load, and a single-pass monolithic finalise took 997 seconds with an 851 MB peak, which means it does not hang on its own.

**25 GB free disk, checked before the run starts.** A long render that fills the disk at 80% leaves you with nothing.

**Assert the frame before spending the hours.** Render one probe segment per layout, extract its first frame, and compare it against the real assets before the pool starts. One version rendered 13 hours overnight and was rejected in the first minute, on the frame. A "the picture is busy so it must be fine" heuristic is not this check: the video behind a missing element is plenty busy.

**Write a state file after every stage**: where the pipeline is, what it produced, the exact next command. Every resume, wake-up and handoff reads that file rather than a runbook from memory. On one morning somebody typed the pipeline's steps by hand and it replayed stages that had already finished.

**Nothing gets killed while a render is running.** Not a session, not the big process, not anything. Pause file sync and close browsers instead. This was paid for twice in one day: an out-of-memory kill took a 4 hour render at 98%, and then a memory-freeing sweep killed its rerun at 95%, because every "free up memory" heuristic targets the largest process and the largest process is always ffmpeg.

**And a machine gets cleared by a person before an overnight run.** An automatic sweep closed **0 of 19** open sessions on the night that mattered, because it is forbidden from touching anything interactive. Only a person can do that part.

## Part 5: the 3 rules under all of it

**1. A correction lands where the next session will see it, in the same session.** A caption floor once lived inside a single episode's build script. It was the right fix. 3 days later the same fault shipped on 12 of 15 renders, because nothing outside that episode could see it. A fix that only touches today's recording says so out loud and says why.

**2. Green is not done.** A clean check battery means the file is now worth a person's time to watch. Done is them having watched it. The battery exists to stop wasting their watches on files with a fixable defect, never to replace the watch. Under an explicit end-to-end mandate too: a finished public artifact is not the kind of irreversible thing an autonomy grant covers.

**3. Catch what a viewer would catch, and ask about taste.** A fault any viewer would notice is yours to fix without asking. A genuine taste call goes to the person, and their answer becomes a line in their taste files, dated, in their words. 2 review passes once recommended shrinking a caption size on corpus grounds and were both wrong, because the person had set that floor by hand 3 times. **A corpus finding never overrides a floor somebody set.**
