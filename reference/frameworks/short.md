# Framework, the short

> What counts as a short here, where they come from, and the one rule about
> format that costs the most to learn late. Craft rules live in your taste
> files: `content/taste/clip-bar.md` (what makes a moment a clip, and how the
> cut lands) and `content/taste/shorts-formats.md` (the safe frame, caption
> floors, crops). This file governs what qualifies and how it gets made.
> Installed at `content/frameworks/short.md`; read by `shorts-editor` and
> `content-waterfall`.

---

## The bar: transformation, not a reframe

A raw cut plus a vertical crop is never a short. A short is a made thing:
cards, captions, pacing, and a first frame that packages the promise.

If the only work done was cropping and trimming, it does not ship. That is the
whole gate, and it is worth holding because the alternative is a feed full of
pieces that look like offcuts, which is what they are.

---

## Where shorts come from

2 sources, both chosen by a person, never auto-selected.

1. **The brief's pre-shaped beats.** When a long-form gets briefed, mark which
   beat of each section is expected to carry the short, usually the analogy or
   the framework, and hold the lines they want verbatim. That is where you look
   first. The picks still come from watching the tape.
2. **The weekly clip sitting.** Once a week, sit and pick the best moments from
   what has launched and what is coming. Taste is never automated. The machine
   assembles what a person picked.

**Cadence is proposed, never decided.** Say what you think the number should be
and why, and let the person running it confirm or overrule. A reduction from
their stated ambition, made quietly on quality grounds, is a decision you took
on their behalf. Put it in front of them.

**A topic you have not used yourself yet gets shorts first.** Learn it, make
a short per thing you learned as you learn it, and stack them. A long form
comes later, only if the stack earns it. Timelier, and it shows which topics
actually land before you spend a long-form on one.

---

## The short's job

Teach something and deliver real value in under 60 seconds. Every format is
judged on that before anything else.

---

## Cut for the best part, not only for the filler

Removing the umms is the easy half. The other half is a creative call:

- **Before a cut is built, name the one thing a viewer saves the short for.**
  Usually the prompt, the number or the line. Make sure it is in, and reach it
  fast. Setup that only leads to it gets cut.
- **A second speaker stays only if they add something new.** When the second
  person repeats what was already said, cut them, unless what they add is
  worth the seconds.
- **A strong moment can be cut twice**, once from each speaker's side.
- Cut review checks this too: is the best line in the cut, and how fast do we
  reach it?

## Craft rules

- **The hook stands alone.** The first seconds complete a problem-to-promise
  arc without the rest of the video existing.
- **First frame is the packaged promise**, a designed card, not a cold talking
  head mid-sentence.
- **Captions are phrase level, sentence case**, built word by word in sync with
  speech, current word highlighted. The 1 to 2 word ALL CAPS style is out.
- **No face in a box.** Full frame speaker, or clean full bleed graphics. Text
  and visuals live where a phone viewer actually looks.
- **Music ducks under voice.** Sound effects punctuate real transitions only.
- **Every visual change carries information.** The cards hold the visual
  argument, the captions hold the words. A card is never text in a box.

## The frame is not a container

The rule, stated plainly: **crop the source to the format, never letterbox the
source into the format.**

A landscape band parked in the middle of a vertical frame, usually over a
blurred copy of itself, is the format being tolerated. A vertical crop that cuts the sides away and fills the frame is the
format being used. This applies to every surface that is natively vertical.

### What it costs, measured

Going vertical means cropping the source **width**, so the upscale factor is
set by the source **height**, not the source width. That changes the arithmetic
completely, and it is the number that decides whether a clip can exist at all.

For a 1080 by 1206 vertical face slot:

| Source | Native | Vertical crop | Upscale | Verdict at 1:1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full frame speaker | 1920 x 1080 | 967 x 1080 | **1.11x** | sharp, skin texture and eyelashes hold |
| Video-call gallery tile | 480 x 270 | 244 x 270 | **4.43x** | mush, fine detail smears |
| Video-call screen-share strip tile | 240 x 134 | 121 x 134 | **8.93x** | gone |

**Judge this at 1:1 pixels, never at preview size.** All 3 look acceptable
scaled to 300px wide. Only the 1:1 crop separates them, and a preview-sized
check is exactly how a blurry short ships.

**What it cost the first time.** On one recording, 9 of the 10 clips a person
picked were from gallery or screen-share tiles, so the vertical rule put them
out of reach of that recording entirely. One clip could be built. The fix lives
back at the shoot: record the source at full frame in the first place.

**A higher bitrate master does not rescue this.** Re-exporting the raw can
reduce compression mush, but it cannot add pixels, so every upscale factor
above is unchanged.

**A landscape ceiling does not survive the vertical rule.** "A 540px inset is
fine" was an answer to a different question, and it was superseded the moment
the format changed.

## Distribution

Wherever vertical video lives, plus cross-posting to a feed where the piece
fits. Titles follow your own short-form title patterns. Caption style follows
your per-surface rules, because the same words do not work on every platform.

---

## Retro

Rules enter from approvals and rejections of finished shorts, dated, with the
short that taught them. A render error that repeats goes in the editor's own
error table instead, because that is a bug and not a taste rule.
