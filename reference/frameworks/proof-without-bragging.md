# Framework, proof without making yourself the subject

> How to carry proof in a piece without it reading as a boast. For a video that
> makes a claim, a pitch, a prep doc, or the live arithmetic on a sales call.
> Installed at `content/frameworks/proof-without-bragging.md`; read by
> `content`, `script-writer` and `post-writer` whenever a piece has to carry
> proof, alongside `content/taste/stories.md`.
>
> Status: the beats and the mechanism held up. **The order has been tested once
> in a live room, and lost.** Read the field test below before applying the
> order.

---

## The problem it solves

Proof is self-referential by nature. "Look what we did" is the only way to say
it, and that is also the thing that makes a listener close up.

The answer here is to spend most of the sequence in the listener's world and
hand them the arithmetic at the end, so the claim becomes theirs rather than
yours.

---

## The beats

1. **The problem story.** A situation with a person in it.
2. **Big numbers.** The scale of the thing.
3. **The example.** The concrete case where it got fixed.
4. **The mechanism.** Why the fix worked. Without it the example is a claim
   taken on faith, which is the version that reads as arrogant, and nothing
   transfers: the listener cannot say "I have that too." It goes **after** the
   example, never before, so the concrete gives the abstraction its meaning.
5. **Their arithmetic.** Pre-prepared from the listener's own shape (team size,
   locations, volume, whatever is public) so it lands as "at a business your
   size, here is what this looks like for you."

---

## The guards

- **Beat 2 is where it tips into a flex.** A number about the industry
  educates. A number about you is a boast in the wrong position. So the scale
  figure is the market's, and your own result stays in beat 3, where it works
  as an answer.
- **Beat 5 is fragile at the exact moment it matters.** If the number leans on
  an assumption the listener does not hold, it breaks while they are leaning
  in. 2 protections:
  - Say the assumption out loud first ("assuming you are near the industry
    average, tell me if you are better"), so they correct you instead of
    dismissing you.
  - Give a range, never a point figure. A range reads as measured. A precise
    projection reads as a pitch deck.
- **Route by business model.** Different ways of charging need different
  arithmetic. Know which one the listener runs before the numbers start.
- **What you can pre-prepare is a frame, not a number.** Public facts give you
  the frame. Internal figures are not public. Completing the number live with
  their input is better anyway, because the participation is what makes it
  theirs.
- **In a live room, beat 2 can come from the room.** A show of hands produces a
  number nobody can argue with, and it removes the need for a statistic you
  have not researched.
- **Keep the odd numbers odd.** Specific figures read as measured, round ones
  read as invented. Write 1,468, not "about 1,500".

## The field test, and what it changed

**Tested in a live 7-minute pitch to a room of about 20. The problem-first
order lost.** About a third of the slides and well over a third of the time
went on the problem before a word about what the product actually was. The
room's feedback: they wanted to know what it is.

The presenter's own read afterwards, in substance: a benefits-first,
problem-first habit had made the presenter forget that people first want to know what the
thing is.

**What this corrects.** The problem story had been put first on the strength of
the identifiable-victim research below. That research does not support
problem-before-product, and reading it that way was an over-read. It says a
story beats a statistic, which is a ruling about beat 1 against beat 2. It says
nothing about whether the audience already knows what you are offering.

**So the order now reads:** when the audience does not yet know what the thing
is, say what it is first, then run the sequence. The beats and the mechanism
are unchanged. Only their position relative to naming the product moves. A
warm reader who already knows the offer can take the problem story cold.

## The research behind it

Searchable by author and title. Read the originals before quoting them on
camera.

- **Story before statistic (beat 1 against beat 2).** Paul Slovic's work on
  psychic numbing and the identifiable-victim effect: as the number of people
  affected rises, emotional response falls. A big number on its own reduces
  response. Numbers carry weight only when attached to an identified case.
- **Name a weakness after showing competence.** Elliot Aronson's pratfall
  effect (1966): a competent person who makes a small slip is rated more
  likeable, while the same slip damages somebody who has not shown competence
  yet. Acknowledging a minor weakness reads as the real story rather than an
  edited one.
- **Proof, promise, plan.** A widely taught sales sequence: proof first to earn
  attention, then the promise, then the plan that removes the objection.
  "Flaunt your flaws" sits in the same family as the pratfall effect.
- **Teach, tailor, take control.** The Challenger Sale (Dixon and Adamson),
  built on a large study of sales professionals. Beats 1 to 4 are teach, beat 5
  is tailor. Worth knowing, because your buyers have likely been sold with it.
- **Specific numbers.** Claude Hopkins, Scientific Advertising (1923): specific
  figures read as measured, round figures read as invented.

## Examples worth tearing down on camera

Analysing other people and brands running this pattern borrows their authority
and proves the framework without claiming anything for yourself. The teardown
is the proof.

- **An industry benchmark report published by a vendor in your market.** Many
  markets have one: a problem story, then the industry's own numbers, then a
  calculator so a reader compares themselves. A vendor that publishes the
  industry's numbers and lets readers do their own arithmetic often holds the
  most trusted position in the market.
- **A personal-finance show that runs real people's numbers on screen**: beat 5
  as entertainment, their own arithmetic run back at them.
- **A news organisation's personalisation piece** (a quiz or a calculator that
  answers "what about me?"). These are routinely among a publication's most
  shared pieces.

Mix institutions and operators. An all-creator lineup reads as creator-economy
talk to a professional audience.
