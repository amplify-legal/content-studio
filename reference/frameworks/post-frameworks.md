# Framework, written posts

> Short written posts on a feed. 2 worked examples of 2 different registers,
> then the shape that holds under both. The 2 examples are **examples**, taken
> from a setup where 2 people post from the same business in voices that have
> almost nothing in common. Replace them with your own. What survives the
> replacement is the part underneath.

---

## Why 2 examples and not 1

Most post frameworks describe one voice and quietly assume it is the right one.
It is usually the long, operator, here-is-what-I-learned voice, because that is
the voice of the person who wrote the framework.

Put 2 registers side by side and you can see which rules belong to the register
and which belong to posting itself. The rules in the last section of this file
are the ones that held in both.

If 2 people post from your business, write one of these per person and never
let a draft cross over. Copy belongs to the account, not to whoever typed it.

---

## Example A, the long-form operator voice

**Who this fits.** Somebody who has built or sold or delivered the thing, is
writing to buyers who lurk and rarely comment, and whose credibility comes from
specifics.

**Cadence in the source setup:** 2 posts a week, one of each shape below.

### The 2 weekly shapes

1. **The buyer-question post.** A real question from a real buyer, asked in a
   sales call, a workshop, a comment, an email. The honest answer, grounded in
   something actually built or sold or delivered. The question is the hook.
2. **The point of view post.** A position, or a story from the week's work,
   told narrative first. Specific, human, a small arc.

### The rules that do not bend

- **Never drafted from the video script.** Posts come from mined language:
  what was actually said on the call, in the recording, in the workshop.
  Transcribe rather than generate. A post assembled out of a script reads like
  a summary of a thing, and a post pulled out of speech reads like a person.
- **Get their story first, then draft.** Before writing a word, get the
  answers: what is your story in this, how did you arrive at it, why did you
  think it mattered. Draft from those answers. Drafting first and asking after
  produces something they then have to correct into existence.
- **No engagement bait.** No comment-the-keyword, no reply-with-a-number.
  Buyers lurk. They read, they do not comment, and they book weeks later. Close
  by giving something real.
- **Full natural sentences.** No fragment chains, no arrow characters, no em
  dashes, emojis rare enough to mean something. Informal register, complete
  sentences.
- **Treat these as first class.** Per hour of effort these often carry more
  buying weight than video, because this is the surface the lurking buyer opens
  every day. They are not repurposing exhaust.

### Hook taxonomy

Pick the hook from the angle of the post. Never collapse this to one shape
because one shape worked once.

**Copywriting frameworks**

- **Problem, agitate, solution.** "A shop we worked with was leaving [X] of
  their [Y] weekly enquiries unanswered. Most came in on the weekend, which is
  exactly when people finally have time to shop around. Every one of those was
  a sale that went to the shop down the road." (An invented illustration with
  placeholders; put your own sourced numbers in.) Every number in a draft
  traces to a real source or it does not go in. Never invent a statistic for a
  hook.
- **Picture, promise, proof, push.** Picture the thing that did not happen.
  Name where it leaks. Give the one real result. Ask, but only in the posts
  whose turn it is to ask.
- **Situation, task, action, result.** The case study shape. Who they were,
  what was broken, what got built, what came back. Lead with the result when
  the number is strong.
- **Attention, interest, desire, action.** For announcements and resources. The
  first line is the hook, always.

**Story hooks**

- **The transformation**, told with the customer anonymous unless you have
  written permission to name them.
- **The honest miss.** The thing you got wrong and what it taught. In the
  source setup, the confession posts carry the best engagement of anything.
- **Objection flip.** Open with the objection verbatim ("we tried this, the
  team hated it"), then tell the story that answers it.
- **Contrarian**, only where you actually disagree. Manufactured friction reads
  as manufactured, and it is the fastest way to sound like everybody else while
  trying to sound different.

**Opening patterns**

Problem call-out. True-cost question. A real verified number. Painting the
picture ("you are still doing this the way you did it in 2019"). A real
conversation with the insight surfaced. The problem I solved. The confession.
The objection as the opening line.

### Readability

Short sentences win. Aim at a reading grade of about 5. If a post reads
produced, it is wrong, however good the argument is.

## Example B, the short-form voice

**Who this fits.** Somebody who writes short, writes fast, and whose posts lose
something every time a machine touches the words.

**The boundary that makes this work:** the machine shapes, the person writes.
Structure, angle, raw material from the week. The words are theirs, always.

### The 2 weekly shapes

1. **The objection flip.** A real objection from the week, from a call, a
   reply, a comment. Opened verbatim, then flipped with what actually happened.
   The machine hands over the objection as heard, the facts that answer it, and
   the number if there is one. They tell it their way.
2. **The personal story.** First draft is theirs by rule. The machine's only
   job is to surface a candidate moment from the week, something they said on a
   call, a thing they shipped, a founder moment, and to offer a shape if they
   ask for one.

### What the machine hands over

For each post, a short note and nothing more:

- the angle, in one line
- the raw material: quotes, numbers, names cleared for use
- a suggested opening pattern from the taxonomy in Example A
- what the piece is for, meaning which bucket of the week's mix it fills

Never a finished draft for shape 2. For shape 1, a draft is allowed only if
they ask for one.

### Boundaries

- Customer facts stay private unless they are already public or cleared.
- Their posts are theirs to schedule and send. Nothing stages without them.
- Warmth matches the actual relationship, never manufactured for the post.

## The shape underneath both

Everything in this section held in both registers, which is the only reason to
trust it. Start here when you write your own.

**1. Posts come from speech, not from documents.** Whatever the register, the
material is something the person actually said out loud. Record your calls,
record yourself thinking, and mine that. A post sourced from a document sounds
sourced from a document.

**2. The interview comes before the draft.** Get their story, then write. In
both registers, the failure mode is identical: a machine writes something
plausible, the person spends 20 minutes making it true, and the result is worse
than if they had been asked 3 questions first.

**3. One idea per post.** If the post has 2 arguments, it is 2 posts.

**4. The mix is deliberate and it is mostly giving.** In the source setup,
roughly 60% of pieces carry no ask at all, 20% point at something free, 20%
point at the thing you sell. Set your own ratio, write it down, and check the
week against it rather than deciding post by post.

**5. No engagement bait in either voice.** It works, which is the problem. The
people it brings are a different audience from the one that buys.

**6. Nothing posts itself.** Drafts and queues are the output. A person presses
the button.

**7. The retro is the part that compounds.** Approved posts go into the
examples file the drafting reads from. Rejected drafts get one line saying why,
dated. A reason that shows up twice is a rule, and it goes in this file the same
day. That loop is the difference between a framework that ages well and a
framework nobody opens after month 2.
