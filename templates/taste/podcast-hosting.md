## Starting defaults, change them

### 2 failures this file exists to prevent

1. **The run sheet was written into chat and never built as a file.** You cannot read scrollback while hosting. The run sheet is a file, handed over, every time.
2. **The format was rebuilt from scratch each episode**, so what worked last time went missing. The format lives here.

### The intro, read off the page as bullet points

At the top of the run sheet, as bullets, never a paragraph. Why: a host who records from a paragraph ends up reading it, and a read intro sounds clunky; one working host asked for bullets after a dozen paragraph intros, and refused a teleprompter as the alternative.

- **1 bullet is 1 breath**, about 6 to 14 words. Glance, look up, say it.
- **4 to 6 bullets per version:** the welcome, the guest's name, their striking fact, their seat, the promise of the episode, the handoff.
- **The first and last bullets are the same in every version.** Only the middle changes, so the host can switch versions mid-open without losing their place.
- **Facts are fragments, not sentences**, where a fragment reads cleanly out loud. Bold the 1 or 2 words that must not be got wrong (the name, the number).
- On the page the block is a list, never a paragraph, at 20px or larger.

A shape that works:

> - <Welcome line with the show name>. I'm <host>.
> - Today's guest: **<full name>**
> - <the one fact or arc that makes a stranger lean in, as a fragment>
> - <their seat: title, company, book if there is one>
> - Today we're talking about <the promise of this episode> (or let the bridge below carry the topic)
> - *"But for now, <first name>, welcome, how are you?"*

- **Lead with the striking thing, not the title.** The credential comes second, as confirmation.
- **The last line never changes.** It is the handoff.
- **3 labelled versions:** primary (uses the guest's own strongest claim), alternate (leads on the human arc), playful (optional, only when it fits).
- **Every fact in the intro is sourced** in a companion file, with one line on the page saying so. Never a fact the guest has not said or published: no inferred seniority, no rounded-up numbers, no "one of the leading".

### The frame

- **Open:** the intro above, as bullets.
- **A show line after the welcome**, if you have one: one sentence on what the show is and who it sits down with, the same every episode.
- **The bridge, before the first question:** one breath on what you do, what the guest works on, and the episode's topic in plain words ("we do X, our guest does Y, here's where we meet in the middle"). It replaces the intro's "today we're talking about" bullet, so the topic is said once. The strongest bridge across many real tapes was one thing the guest said before the recording, in their words, that made the host want them on.
- **First question:** <your default opener, for example "for people who don't know you, what are you known for, and what excites you right now?">
- **The middle flows.** 4 to 6 thought-out questions from the intro call's notes, each written as it would be said, each naming who asks if there are 2 hosts. They are a safety net, not a script, and not read in order.
- **Never "I believe" in front of a fact.** A question that carries a fact states it plainly and hands it to the guest to confirm: "You spent 8 years inside a firm before this. Is that accurate?", never "I believe you spent 8 years...". Why: the hedge puts doubt in the host's own words, while the question lets the guest correct it, and a correction on air often becomes a warm moment.
- **Close, in order:** 2 or 3 deeper, more personal questions chosen per guest near the end (your standing question, word for word, is the first of them), then "where do people find you?" with the exact handle or URL written on the page, then the call to action both sides agreed on.

### The opener, beat by beat (a starting structure, guidelines more than a script)

Only the lines you mark word for word are said the same way every episode. On real tapes the whole open ran about 90 seconds, welcome to first question.

- **Beat 0, before record, off tape.** Read the guest's name, show and website back to them, and ask where people should find them. Say the shape in one breath (who opens, who introduces, then it is a conversation). Say what you want from them. Give them the pause button, and calm a first-timer ("it's a conversation"). A second take of the open is normal, and the second take is plainer.
- **Beat 1, the welcome, about 20 seconds.** The same first line every episode. Name the co-host, with one line on what they bring. Your own line, one breath, in a form you can source. Then "today we have a very special guest".
- **Beat 2, the handoff to the co-host** (if there is one), one fixed line, for example "But for now, who is our special guest?", which the co-host answers with the guest's name.
- **Beat 3, the guest introduction, under a minute.** Lead with the striking thing, not the title. A number you are unsure of goes to the guest as a question. Re-read the bio the morning of the recording: guests correct stale facts on air. Keep it short; guests notice a 2-minute intro that is a pitch. Close it with the fixed handoff line, and both hosts answer the hello.
- **Beat 4, the bridge** (above).
- **Beat 5, the first question:** one breath, then stop. A long introduction and question run together lose the guest ("I almost forgot what the original question is"). When the research holds a story they have told in public, it can be the first question instead.
- **A hook in front of the welcome is the edit's job**, not something said in the room: the trailer cut from the guest's best lines (`content/reference/podcast-edit.md`).

**Never in the open:** a pitch for yourself before the guest has spoken, a fact nobody checked, "I believe" in front of a fact.

### The anatomy (every block must change what you say or do in the room)

1. **Blockers, only if live.** Numbered actions with owners. Omit when there are none.
2. **Why this guest said yes**, one sentence. What the episode has to deliver for them.
3. **Status strip:** accept status, start time, hard stop, name pronunciation.
4. **The link**, big enough to read at arm's length.
5. **The intro**, as bullets, in 3 labelled versions.
6. **Who they are**, 6 rows maximum.
7. **The run sheet:** timed beats, each question written out verbatim, what they already said so you follow up rather than ask, the beat most likely to overrun.
8. **The new material:** the 1 or 2 questions never answered anywhere else.
9. **3 don'ts** specific to this guest.
10. **Their best lines** from the intro call, to quote back.
11. **Clips to flag** for the edit.
12. **After the call:** what to send, update, decide.

### The cut test

A block whose label names what it IS gets cut or renamed to what you DO with it. If a block triggers no action in the room, it goes to the companion file. Too much text and undefined sections fail when read live, which is the only test that counts.

### Type and delivery

- 18px floor, beats at 19 to 22px. It is read at arm's length while talking.
- Delivered as a file as early as the facts allow, never minutes before.
- **A replacement supersedes its predecessor in the same turn.** Rename the old one `_superseded-<name>`. Two readable docs for one recording is the failure.

### Guest mode (when you are the guest)

You do not control the arc, so do not build one. The kit: your first 60 seconds written verbatim (same 3-version, sourced discipline), a story bank with the numbers and anonymity already applied, the 2 or 3 lines you want to exist as clips, and a do-not-say list with replacement wording. Research the show, not the host: what it has already covered decides what you add.

### Ask every guest before the record

1. What distribution do they have?
2. What is our call to action into their audience?
3. What call to action do they want shown for them, with the exact handle or URL?
